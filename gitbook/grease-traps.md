# Grease Traps: A Champion Plumbing Reference Guide

## Grease traps in Edmond

Grease traps, known as FOG interceptors or grease interceptors, function as plumbing devices placed between kitchen drain lines and the municipal sewer system. These devices capture fats, oils, and grease prior to entry into the sanitary sewer. A grease trap retains wastewater for a sufficient period so that FOG cools, separates from the water, and rises to the surface, where baffles contain the layer while cleaner water departs through the outlet pipe. ### Parts of a grease trap

A grease trap consists of five core components that operate together to separate FOG from wastewater. The inlet pipe delivers wastewater from kitchen fixtures into the trap body. The inlet baffle slows incoming flow so solids settle at the bottom and grease rises to the surface instead of continuing through the unit. The outlet baffle retains the floating FOG layer. Treated water leaves the device through the outlet pipe. The trap body serves as the containment vessel and holds the volume required for separation to take place before the water proceeds. ### Measurable attributes: capacity, flow rate and material

Grease traps possess three measurable attributes that decide whether a unit receives correct sizing for a specific kitchen. Capacity holds primary importance. Flow rate, expressed in gallons per minute (GPM), determines the speed at which wastewater may enter the trap while still permitting enough retention time for separation. Material controls durability and installation suitability: stainless steel units accommodate high-temperature discharge along with heavy commercial use, whereas plastic units fit lighter-duty or below-ground residential applications.

### How grease traps relate to the broader plumbing system

Grease traps occupy a defined position in the drainage sequence: downstream from kitchen fixtures and upstream from the sanitary sewer connection. In the absence of a working FOG interceptor, fats and oils cool inside the sewer line, solidify, and produce drain blockage capable of backing up into the building or obstructing the municipal main. A grease trap differs from a septic tank. A septic tank processes all household wastewater by means of biological decomposition and soil absorption, whereas a grease interceptor addresses only FOG at the source prior to any larger system. Grease traps maintain no link to the storm drain or storm sewer system; storm drains convey surface runoff through an entirely separate network. ### Common grease trap problems and warning signs

Grease traps exhibit predictable failure when maintenance schedules lapse or the unit receives undersizing relative to actual kitchen output. A full trap loses separation effectiveness and allows grease to travel downstream into the sewer line, where it hardens and creates blockage. Slow drains across the kitchen appear as the initial indicator; a sulfur or rotten-egg odor near the trap develops next as trapped organic matter breaks down. Visible grease rising into floor drains or sinks indicates the trap has exceeded capacity.

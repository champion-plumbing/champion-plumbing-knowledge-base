Grease traps, called FOG interceptors or grease interceptors, are plumbing devices installed between kitchen drain lines and the municipal sewer system. They intercept fats, oils, and grease before those substances enter the sanitary sewer. A grease trap holds wastewater long enough for FOG to cool, separate from the water, and float to the surface, where baffles hold it in place while cleaner water flows out through the outlet pipe. Parts of a grease trap

A grease trap is made up of five core components that work together to separate FOG from wastewater. The inlet pipe carries wastewater from kitchen fixtures into the trap body. Inside, the inlet baffle slows the flow so solids settle to the bottom and grease floats to the surface rather than passing straight through. The outlet baffle keeps the floating FOG layer in place. Treated water then exits through the outlet pipe. The trap body itself is the containment vessel, sized to hold enough volume for separation to occur before the water moves on. Measurable attributes: capacity, flow rate and material

Grease traps carry three measurable attributes that determine whether a unit is correctly sized for a given kitchen. Capacity matters most. Flow rate, measured in gallons per minute (GPM), sets how fast wastewater can enter the trap while still allowing adequate retention time for separation. Material governs durability and installation context: stainless steel units handle high-temperature discharge and heavy commercial use, while plastic units suit lighter-duty or below-ground residential applications.

How grease traps relate to the broader plumbing system

Grease traps sit at a specific point in the drainage chain: downstream of kitchen fixtures and upstream of the sanitary sewer connection. Without a functioning FOG interceptor, fats and oils cool inside the sewer line, solidify and cause drain blockage that can back up into the building or create blockages in the municipal main. A grease trap is not a septic tank. A septic tank handles all household wastewater through biological decomposition and soil absorption, while a grease interceptor targets only FOG at the source before it reaches any larger system. Grease traps have no connection to the storm drain or storm sewer system; storm drains carry surface runoff and are a separate network entirely. For facilities that need hot-water appliances on the same plumbing system, water heaters are a related component that affects the temperature of discharge entering the trap. High-temperature discharge can liquefy grease and push it past the baffle, which is one reason tankless water heaters with precise temperature control are sometimes specified alongside commercial grease interceptors. Common grease trap problems and warning signs

Grease traps fail in predictable ways when maintenance intervals are missed or the unit is undersized for actual kitchen output. A full trap stops separating FOG effectively, sending grease downstream into the sewer line where it solidifies and causes blockage. Slow drains throughout the kitchen are the first sign; a sulfur or rotten-egg odor near the trap follows as trapped organic matter decomposes.

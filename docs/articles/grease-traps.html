Grease Traps: A Champion Plumbing Reference Guide

Updated November 2026

Grease traps in Edmond

Grease traps, known as FOG interceptors or grease interceptors, function as plumbing devices placed between kitchen drain lines and the municipal sewer system. They capture fats, oils, and grease before those materials reach the sanitary sewer. A grease trap retains wastewater long enough for FOG to cool, separate from the water, and rise to the surface, where baffles contain it while cleaner water departs through the outlet pipe. Parts of a grease trap

A grease trap consists of five core components that operate together to separate FOG from wastewater. The inlet pipe delivers wastewater from kitchen fixtures into the trap body. Inside, the inlet baffle reduces flow velocity so solids settle to the bottom and grease rises to the surface instead of continuing through. The outlet baffle holds the floating FOG layer in position. Treated water leaves through the outlet pipe. The trap body serves as the containment vessel and is sized to provide sufficient volume for separation to take place before the water proceeds. Measurable attributes: capacity, flow rate and material

Grease traps possess three measurable attributes that decide whether a unit fits a given kitchen. Capacity holds primary importance. Flow rate, expressed in gallons per minute (GPM), determines how quickly wastewater may enter the trap while still permitting enough retention time for separation. Material controls durability and installation setting: stainless steel units manage high-temperature discharge and heavy commercial use, while plastic units fit lighter-duty or below-ground residential applications.

How grease traps relate to the broader plumbing system

Grease traps occupy a defined location in the drainage sequence: downstream from kitchen fixtures and upstream from the sanitary sewer connection. Without an operational FOG interceptor, fats and oils cool inside the sewer line, harden and produce drain blockage that can back up into the building or obstruct the municipal main. A grease trap differs from a septic tank. A septic tank processes all household wastewater through biological decomposition and soil absorption, while a grease interceptor addresses only FOG at the source before it enters any larger system. Grease traps maintain no link to the storm drain or storm sewer system; storm drains handle surface runoff and form an entirely separate network. For facilities that require hot-water appliances on the same plumbing system, water heaters represent a related component that influences the temperature of discharge entering the trap. High-temperature discharge can liquefy grease and force it beyond the baffle, which explains why tankless water heaters with precise temperature control appear alongside commercial grease interceptors in some specifications. Common grease trap problems and warning signs

Grease traps fail in predictable patterns when maintenance intervals lapse or the unit lacks sufficient size for actual kitchen output. A full trap ceases to separate FOG effectively and sends grease onward into the sewer line where it hardens and creates blockage. Slow drains across the kitchen appear as the initial indicator; a sulfur or rotten-egg odor near the trap develops as trapped organic matter breaks down. Visible grease rising into floor drains or sinks indicates the trap has exceeded capacity. Grease trap installation and maintenance in commercial kitchens

Under-sink grease traps handle single fixtures in smaller operations. Large in-ground interceptors serve entire commercial kitchens and involve excavation plus concrete work. Maintenance adheres to a pumping schedule determined by the trap's capacity and the kitchen's FOG output.

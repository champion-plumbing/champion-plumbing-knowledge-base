Backflow Prevention Standards: A Champion Plumbing Reference Guide

Updated November 2026

Backflow prevention standards in Edmond, OK

Backflow Prevention Standards, called Backflow Codes or Cross-Connection Control Regulations, form the set of plumbing rules that set design, installation, testing and maintenance requirements for devices preventing contaminated water from reversing direction into a clean supply. These standards determine which devices receive approval for each hazard level, establish required test intervals, identify who may perform the tests and specify what records must reach the local water authority. Organizations such as the American Society of Sanitary Engineering (ASSE) and the American Water Works Association (AWWA) issue the model codes that most municipalities, including Edmond, adopt and enforce.

Scope of backflow prevention standards

Backflow Prevention Standards cover every location where a potable water supply joins a non-potable source, a connection known in the industry as a cross-connection. Residential properties, commercial buildings, irrigation systems, fire suppression lines and industrial process piping all fall under these rules. The standards separate two hazard classifications: a health hazard, where backflow could introduce substances dangerous to human health, and a non-health hazard, where the risk stays limited to cosmetic or minor quality issues. The device required at any cross-connection follows that hazard classification alone, not pipe size or building age.

Governing bodies and versions

Backflow Codes receive authority from several overlapping organizations. ASSE publishes product performance standards. ASSE 1013 covers reduced-pressure zone assemblies, ASSE 1015 covers double-check valve assemblies, and ASSE 1020 covers pressure vacuum breakers, among others. AWWA publishes M14, the manual of cross-connection control, which guides utility programs. The International Plumbing Code (IPC) and the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) each incorporate backflow requirements by reference, and states adopt one or the other with local amendments. Requirements can vary and should be confirmed with the local building authority. Municipalities add their own cross-connection control programs, so the version in force in Edmond may differ from the version in a neighboring city even within the same state. Testing frequency and compliance requirements

Non-compliance with Backflow Preventer Regulations carries real consequences. Regulatory violations expose property owners to liability if a backflow event contaminates a neighboring supply. A property equipped with a tested and certified assembly stands apart from one operating with unregulated water flow. The difference separates a protected supply from a public health risk. Backflow testing and repair work requires a licensed professional in nearly every case, and test reports often must reach the local water authority to maintain property compliance.

Device specifications under backflow codes

Backflow Prevention Standards list which assembly types receive approval for each hazard level and installation condition. Reduced-pressure zone assemblies become required at high-hazard cross-connections. They contain two independently operating check valves plus a differential pressure relief valve that opens if either check fails. Double-check valve assemblies receive approval for low-hazard applications such as a standard residential irrigation connection. Atmospheric vacuum breakers and pressure vacuum breakers hold narrower approval. They remain permitted only where the device stays free from continuous pressure or downstream valves that could hold pressure against it.

Relationship to the broader plumbing system

Backflow Prevention Standards occupy a defined place inside the larger set of plumbing regulations that protect water quality from the treatment plant to the tap. Cross-connection control forms the field that backflow codes protect; without it, a pressure drop anywhere in the distribution system, caused by a water main break, heavy firefighting demand or a pump failure, can draw contaminants from a garden hose, a chemical tank or a boiler back into the public supply. Common signs of backflow prevention standards non-compliance

Backflow Prevention Standards violations do not always appear obvious, yet several conditions indicate that a property's cross-connection control program has lapsed or was never put in place. A missing annual test report ranks as the most common finding. The assembly may sit physically present yet remain untested, which registers as non-compliance under most jurisdiction programs. A failed differential pressure test indicates one or both check valves inside the assembly fail to hold the required pressure differential and leave the supply unprotected. Improper device selection occurs when a low-hazard assembly such as a double-check valve appears at a high-hazard cross-connection, a mismatch that violates the device specification requirements of the applicable backflow code. Inaccessible assemblies, buried, enclosed in a locked vault without a testing port, or installed with insufficient clearance, block annual testing and produce an automatic compliance failure.

Backflow testing, repair and installation for residential and commercial properties typically involves identifying the correct assembly for a given hazard classification, completing the test report and coordinating with the local water authority, all of which support continued property compliance with backflow prevention standards.
